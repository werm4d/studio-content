## Bottom Line Up Front

The question as framed — "SmartRock as SIP infiller per the article concept" — collapses three different technical questions that need separate answers.

**On SmartRock specifically:** It is the wrong product for what the article describes. SmartRock is an interior-side continuous insulation product designed for mass walls (concrete and masonry), not for exterior cavity-wall use over wood-framed sheathing, and not for SIP cores. The article's concept is sound but it requires Rockwool Comfortboard 80/110 or CavityRock — different SKUs in the same product family.

**On the article's concept (mineral wool exterior CI with squash-block furring):** Building-science-defensible, well-tested in cold-climate Passive House practice, and technically correct as written. Useful as a future Merge4Ward product offering and immediately useful as an upgrade path on Claymont's exterior envelope.

**On mineral wool as SIP CORE replacement (between OSB skins):** Structurally different question entirely. Would void Acme Panel ESR-4689 conditions of use for the existing Claymont SIP specification. No US manufacturer currently produces structural mineral-wool-core SIPs with US-recognized evaluation reports. Could be a Phase 4-5 Merge4Ward product development opportunity, not an off-the-shelf substitution.

**On Claymont specifically:** Adding exterior CavityRock between the SIP outer skin and the existing furring channel is a viable envelope upgrade that closes the IECC compliance gap flagged in A-502 review comment #24, addresses the basement-to-above-grade thermal continuity gap flagged in comment #18, and aligns with the project's sustainability narrative — all without disturbing the structural SIP specification.

## 1. SmartRock as a Product — What It Actually Is

ROCKWOOL Smartrock® launched April 2025 and was made available nationwide across the United States in February 2026. It consists of two layers laminated together: a semi-rigid stone wool board (compliant with ASTM C612) and Pro Clima INTELLO PLUS smart vapor retarder membrane bonded to one face.

**Product specifications:**

- 24" × 48" board size
- Thicknesses: 2" to 6" in half-inch increments (in-stock thicknesses 2.5" and 4")
- R-value approximately R-4.3 per inch (so the 2.5" board is ~R-10.7)
- ICC-ES ESR-5374 (the formal evaluation report — note that General Insulation's tech sheet references ESR-5039, which appears to be either an earlier version or a parallel approval for a related product variant; clarify with Rockwool sales before specifying)
- Class A flame spread and smoke development rating per ASTM E84
- Noncombustible per IBC Section 703.3.1
- INTELLO PLUS vapor permeance: 0.09 to >6 perms (humidity-dependent — variable retarder)

**The application SmartRock is designed for:**

Interior continuous insulation on the inside face of concrete and masonry exterior walls. The use case Rockwool's marketing emphasizes is mass-wall retrofit — historic masonry buildings in cities like New York where exterior facade modification is impossible (landmark protection, historic preservation, party wall conditions) but the building owner needs to meet thermal codes like NYC Local Law 97 or move toward Passive House performance. SmartRock is installed inboard of the masonry, glued or fastened directly to the substrate, with TESCON VANA tape sealing the INTELLO PLUS face for airtightness.

**Two important constraints that get missed in the marketing:**

First, Rockwool explicitly recommends *against* adding batt insulation between interior studs in walls already using SmartRock as interior CI. Adding insulation inboard of the smart membrane shifts the temperature and humidity gradient through the assembly, which can cause the variable vapor permeance to behave unpredictably. The product wants to be the only insulation in its plane.

Second, SmartRock is for mass walls — concrete and masonry. The integrated INTELLO PLUS vapor retarder is calibrated to mass-wall hygrothermal behavior. Putting SmartRock against wood-framed sheathing changes the moisture dynamics in ways the product wasn't engineered for, and it would not be an approved use under the ESR.

**Why SmartRock is not the right product for the article's concept:**

The article describes exterior continuous insulation over wood-framed sheathing — outboard of the structural element, with the rockwool serving as both thermal insulation and (effectively) the exterior drainage plane. That is a fundamentally different application than what SmartRock is designed for. The correct Rockwool products for the article's described buildup are:

- **Rockwool Comfortboard 80** — rigid stone wool board for exterior continuous insulation in residential and light commercial. Designed exactly for the article's use case.
- **Rockwool Comfortboard 110** — higher-density version for commercial applications.
- **Rockwool CavityRock** — designed for exterior cavity-wall and rainscreen applications. The article actually names this product correctly.
- **Thermafiber RainBarrier** (Owens Corning, not Rockwool) — also named in the article, also correct for the application.

**Bottom line on SmartRock:** It is a real, well-engineered product, but it is solving a different problem than the article is describing. If Merge4Ward or Aubyn wants to use the article's concept on a future project, the spec is Comfortboard 80/110 or CavityRock — not SmartRock. SmartRock has its own legitimate use cases (basement interior CI, mass-wall retrofit, party-wall blindside CI) but exterior cavity-wall CI over wood framing is not one of them.

## 2. The Article's Concept on Its Own Merits

Setting SmartRock aside, the article describes a specific exterior wall buildup that deserves evaluation as a building-science proposition.

**The buildup, layer by layer (interior to exterior):**

1. 2×6 wood frame with R-20 batt cavity insulation
2. Wood structural sheathing (OSB or plywood)
3. Air/vapor barrier (#15 felt as named in the article, or a more contemporary WRB)
4. Mineral wool exterior CI (CavityRock or RainBarrier, 2"–4" depending on R-value target)
5. Squash blocks (1.5" × 1.5" wood blocks, cut into the rockwool on a grid pattern, oriented with end grain bearing)
6. SIP screws (TruFast SIPTP or equivalent) penetrating from outside the furring through the squash block, the rockwool, the WRB, the sheathing, and 1.5" minimum into wood framing
7. Vertical 2×2 furring at the squash block grid spacing
8. Siding fastened to the 2×2 furring

**R-value summary per the article (correct as written):**

| Rockwool thickness | R-value of CI | Total wall R-value (with R-20 cavity) | Screw length |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2" | R-8 | R-28 | 5.5" |
| 3" | R-12 | R-32 | 6.5" |
| 4" | R-16 | R-36 | 7.5" |

**Building-science evaluation:**

The buildup is sound. Joe Lstiburek and Building Science Corporation have advocated for variants of this approach for over a decade in cold-climate Passive House and high-performance residential practice. The squash-block-and-furring detail correctly addresses the one technical challenge that distinguishes mineral wool from rigid foam at exterior CI applications — mineral wool's lower compressive strength.

**Where the article gets it right:**

- *The compression problem is real.* Driving siding screws directly through 2"-4" of rockwool would compress the insulation under cladding load and create deflection problems at the siding, plus accelerate insulation degradation around fastener points. Squash blocks at 24" o.c. distribute the load to discrete bearing points and leave 99%+ of the rockwool uncompressed.
- *The screw spec is correct.* TruFast SIPTP (or equivalent SIP screws — Simpson SDWS, GRK FastenMaster, etc.) provides the reach required to penetrate 1.5" minimum into wood framing through the full assembly thickness. The article's screw lengths (5.5", 6.5", 7.5" for 2", 3", 4" rockwool) check out.
- *The drainage plane logic is correct.* RainBarrier and CavityRock are specifically engineered to resist water absorption (RainBarrier absorbs only 0.03% moisture by volume per the manufacturer) while remaining vapor-open (perm rating 50). Water sheets off the outer face of the rockwool rather than penetrating it, making the rockwool itself function as a drainage plane. The WRB inboard of the rockwool is a redundant secondary drainage plane against the sheathing — belt and suspenders, but defensible.
- *The performance claims are correct.* Compared to rigid foam (EPS, polyiso, XPS), mineral wool offers superior fire resistance, no off-gassing, no chemical disposal concerns, better acoustic performance (typically +3 to +5 STC at the same thickness), and is pest-resistant.

**Where the article is incomplete or worth pushing on:**

- *The vapor barrier strategy needs more thought.* The article specifies #15 felt as the WRB inboard of the rockwool. Felt is vapor-semi-permeable (around 5 perms). For a vapor-open exterior assembly with mineral wool outboard, the inboard membrane should ideally be a vapor-permeable WRB (e.g. Pro Clima Solitex Mento, Tyvek CommercialWrap) rather than felt. In Climate Zone 4A (Wilmington), this matters less than in colder zones, but it is worth specifying a higher-perm WRB to keep the assembly drying capacity working in both directions.
- *The fastener pull-out at 7.5" embedment with 4" rockwool is structurally workable but not trivial.* The article doesn't address the lever-arm calculation. A 4" exterior rockwool layer creates a 4" moment arm for wind suction loads on the cladding. Engineering judgment is required at higher exposure categories or coastal conditions; the article's "the assembly should hang off the sheathing and support the siding" is correct in principle but understates what the structural calc looks like at the perimeter and at corners. For Wilmington Exposure B / 90 mph design wind (Claymont's design basis), the article's assembly is well within standard fastener pull-out values — but at Exposure C or higher, the 24" o.c. furring spacing may need to tighten.
- *The thermal bridging at fasteners is real but small.* SIP screws are steel, with thermal conductivity orders of magnitude higher than mineral wool. At 24" × 24" spacing, the fastener thermal bridge reduces effective R-value by approximately 3-5% compared to the nominal R-value the article quotes. Not catastrophic, but R-16 nominal becomes R-15 to R-15.5 effective.

**Bottom line on the concept:** Real, defensible, well-tested in similar applications elsewhere. Worth using as a Merge4Ward exterior wall product specification, and worth offering as an upgrade option on Claymont and future projects. Not a SIP-core substitution — that is a different question, addressed below.

## 3. Mineral Wool as SIP Core — A Different Question Entirely

The article's concept places mineral wool *outboard* of the structural wall element. The implicit question in Craig's framing — using mineral wool *inside* the SIP, between the OSB skins, in place of the EPS or polyiso foam core — is structurally a fundamentally different proposition.

**Why the SIP-core substitution is not field-modifiable:**

A Structural Insulated Panel is a composite structural element. The OSB skins and the foam core work together as an integral unit, similar to a beam web-and-flange relationship. The foam core does three structural things:

1. *Distributes compression loads.* The foam core resists the compressive forces between the two OSB skins under axial and transverse loading, preventing the skins from buckling toward each other.
2. *Transfers shear between skins.* The foam adheres to both skins via the structural laminating adhesive. Shear stress from wind, seismic, or eccentric loading transfers through the core.
3. *Provides lateral stability.* The foam keeps the OSB skins parallel and prevents in-plane buckling.

The structural evaluation in ICC-ES ESR-4689 (Acme Panel) assumes a specific core material — EPS, XPS, or polyisocyanurate, depending on the product line — with documented compressive strength, shear modulus, and adhesion characteristics. Substituting mineral wool changes all three properties materially:

- Mineral wool compressive strength is roughly 1/3 to 1/2 that of EPS at equivalent thickness
- Mineral wool does not bond to OSB the way foam does (no chemical adhesion through expansion)
- Mineral wool's shear modulus is materially lower than rigid foam

Field-substituting mineral wool in an Acme Panel SIP would void the conditions of use under which ESR-4689 grants the structural evaluation. The panel would no longer be a code-recognized structural product. This is not "the AHJ might raise an eyebrow" territory — this is "the panel ceases to have a valid structural rating" territory.

**Are there mineral-wool-core structural panels currently produced?**

In Europe, yes. Gutex makes wood-fiber-cored panels and there are multiple European producers of mineral-wool-cored prefab panels under various ETA approvals. ZÜBLIN's LENO-ADD (covered in the prior strategic memo) uses a CLT structural core with mineral wool insulation outboard rather than as the core itself, so it is not a direct comparison.

In the United States, there is no producer with US ESR coverage offering structural mineral-wool-core SIPs at scale for residential structural use. There are some specialty producers operating in commercial roof and wall applications, but the residential SIP market is dominated by EPS/polyiso foam-core panels.

**The Claymont implication is clear and constraining:**

For Claymont specifically, the SIP envelope is already locked in via the Acme Panel ESR-4689 specification. Drawings have been issued, panels are in fabrication, the foundation permit is submitted. Substitution of the SIP core is not a feasible pivot at this stage. The Acme Panel SIPs that arrive on site will be EPS-core or polyiso-core panels, as specified, and the project will proceed with that envelope as designed.

What IS feasible at Claymont is the *outboard* mineral wool addition — discussed in Section 4 below.

## 4. Claymont-Specific Application — Exterior Mineral Wool CI as Envelope Upgrade

The article's exterior mineral wool concept can be applied to Claymont as an envelope upgrade, layered outboard of the existing SIP specification, without disturbing the structural design or the panel fabrication that is already in motion.

**Current Claymont exterior wall buildup (from A-502 detail sheet):**

Interior to exterior:
1. 1/2" gypsum board (interior finish)
2. 1-1/2" metal runner with 2 layers 1" Densglass inside H-studs @ 24" o.c. (fire-rated interior layer, end walls)
3. 6-1/2" SIP wall panel with R-21 (Acme Panel)
4. Moisture barrier
5. Furring channel
6. Fiber cement board siding

**Proposed envelope upgrade buildup:**

Interior to exterior:
1. 1/2" gypsum board
2. 1-1/2" metal runner with 2 layers 1" Densglass inside H-studs @ 24" o.c.
3. 6-1/2" SIP wall panel with R-21
4. **Vapor-permeable WRB (Pro Clima Solitex Mento or Tyvek CommercialWrap) directly against SIP outer skin** — replaces the moisture barrier in the current detail
5. **Rockwool CavityRock or Comfortboard 80, 2" or 3" thick (R-8 to R-12)** — new layer
6. **Furring channel mechanically fastened through CavityRock to SIP outer OSB skin with structural screws** — modified from existing detail
7. Fiber cement board siding (unchanged)

**Why this works without disturbing the SIP structural specification:**

The SIP itself is unmodified — same 6-1/2" panel, same Acme Panel ESR-4689 conditions of use, same structural evaluation in effect. The exterior CI is layered onto the panel face after structural installation. The furring channel takes the same load it was already taking; the only change is that the channel now passes through 2"-3" of rockwool to land on the SIP face instead of landing directly. The squash-block detail from the article is not necessary because the furring channel is a continuous bearing strip rather than a discrete fastener — it transfers load directly to the SIP skin without compressing the rockwool around it.

**What this changes on the rest of the assembly:**

- Total wall R-value increases from R-21 (current) to R-29 (with 2" CavityRock) or R-33 (with 3" CavityRock).
- Wall thickness increases by 2"-3", which means slightly larger trim returns at windows and doors. This is detail-level work, not assembly-level redesign.
- The basement-to-above-grade thermal continuity gap flagged in A-502 review comment #18 is closed cleanly, because the exterior rockwool runs continuously past the rim joist condition and aligns with the basement interior rigid insulation strategy from Detail 1.
- The IECC Climate Zone 4A prescriptive compliance question raised in A-502 review comment #24 is resolved. Total wall R-29 to R-33 well exceeds the prescriptive R-20 + R-5 ci requirement.

**What this does not change:**

- The SIP fabrication scope at Acme. Panels arrive as currently specified.
- The party wall and end wall fire-rating documentation (ratings established for the 6-1/2" SIP and the H-stud assembly remain intact; the exterior CI is outboard of the rated layer).
- The structural EOR's calculations for the SIP envelope. C&C wind pressures on the SIP panel are unchanged because the rockwool is non-structural exterior CI.
- The fiber cement siding manufacturer, fastener spec, or installation method.

**Cost implications (rough order):**

CavityRock 2" runs approximately $1.50-$2.00 per square foot installed in single-family residential. At Claymont's approximately 3,200 SF of exterior wall area (four units × ~800 SF perimeter wall each), the additional material cost is approximately $5,000-$7,000. Labor for the additional layer adds approximately $4,000-$6,000. Total envelope upgrade cost: approximately $10,000-$13,000 across all four units — roughly $2,500-$3,250 per unit.

**Whether to do it on Claymont:**

This is a project-budget decision, not a technical decision. The technical case is sound. The schedule impact is minimal — installing exterior rockwool adds approximately 2-3 days of crew time in the exterior envelope sequence. The grant narrative impact is meaningful — being able to say "Claymont achieves total wall R-33 via exterior continuous mineral wool insulation, exceeding 2018 IECC prescriptive requirements while eliminating rigid foam from the building envelope" is a stronger story for the Wells Fargo / Enterprise application, the OED engagement, and any future Strategic Fund or DDD funding conversations.

**My recommendation:** Specify the envelope upgrade as an additive alternate in the Claymont construction documents. If the budget supports it at the GMP stage, include it. If the budget is tight, hold it as a future upgrade.

## 5. Implications for Merge4Ward Phase 2/3 Product Roadmap

The technology described in the article is genuinely interesting as a Merge4Ward future product line, but the right framing matters.

**Phase 1 — no change.** Merge4Ward Phase 1 is panelized SIP and CLT manufacturing using existing US-evaluated structural products (Acme Panel SIPs or equivalent with ESR coverage; TerraLam TL500S CLT or equivalent under PRG 320). The mineral-wool-core SIP idea is not a Phase 1 product.

**Phase 2 (Year 2-3) — exterior mineral wool wall package as a standard offering.** Merge4Ward could productize the article's concept as a standard Merge4Ward exterior wall package — "Merge4Ward Wall Plus" or similar — combining SIP structural panels with factory-attached or job-site-assembled exterior mineral wool CI, vapor-permeable WRB, furring, and pre-finished siding. This becomes a parallel offering to the standard SIP wall (which gives builders the cheaper, faster-delivery option) and the upgraded wall (which gives builders the higher-R, fire-resistant, vapor-open option for high-performance projects, Passive House work, or grant-eligible deep retrofits). The cost premium is real but the market for it is growing and Merge4Ward is well-positioned to capture it.

**Phase 3 (Year 3-4) — mineral wool factory pre-assembly.** As production capacity matures, Merge4Ward could move the exterior CI installation from job site to factory, attaching CavityRock to the SIP outer skin in the panel assembly area, with WRB and furring pre-installed. Panels arrive on site with the exterior CI already mounted. This is the LENO-ADD-equivalent direction discussed in the prior ZÜBLIN strategic memo. It captures the labor margin from the field installation, and it tightens the quality control on the exterior assembly. Logistics constraint: factory-pre-assembled panels are larger and heavier, requiring more transport capacity and potentially more crane time on site, so the delivered panel size envelope needs evaluation against 53'×8'-6" flatbed limits.

**Phase 4-5 (Year 4-5+) — mineral-wool-core structural panel development.** This is the high-risk, high-reward category. The European precedent exists (multiple ETA-approved producers). The US market gap is real (no current producer with US ESR coverage). The development pathway involves independent structural testing, ICC-ES evaluation report submission, multi-year regulatory cycle, and ideally a partnership with an established European producer (ZÜBLIN's LENO-ADD model is the closest analog). Total development cost is likely $1.5M-$3M before first commercial production. This is a Year 4-5 R&D commitment, not a Year 1-2 product. It would be the kind of capability that justifies Wells Fargo / Enterprise grant follow-on funding under research and product development categories rather than direct construction operations.

**Strategic implication for Merge4Ward positioning:**

The article's concept and the broader mineral-wool-as-envelope-substrate idea aligns with three of Merge4Ward's existing strategic narratives:

- *Sustainability and lifecycle.* Mineral wool eliminates the foam-core disposal question, the off-gassing question, and the fire-load question that rigid-foam SIPs carry. For grant-funded affordable housing where 30-year lifecycle costs and end-of-life disposal increasingly matter, mineral-wool-cored panels are a stronger story.
- *Fire and pest resistance.* In urban infill conditions like Claymont (zero-lot-line, attached units, existing adjacent structures with unknown fire history), the fire-resistance advantage of mineral wool over polyiso is meaningful.
- *Workforce and craft training.* The squash-block-and-furring detail is craft-driven labor — measurable by a trainee, learnable in a few days, and directly usable on site. It fits the LEEP DE workforce training narrative better than highly automated foam-core SIP production does.

Moving toward mineral-wool-integrated wall systems over the Phase 2-5 horizon is a coherent strategic direction that compounds with the rest of the Merge4Ward narrative. Phase 1 stays focused on existing US-evaluated SIP and CLT products to get to revenue. The mineral-wool product line layers in over time as the manufacturing operation matures and as US regulatory infrastructure for novel core materials catches up.

## 6. Connection to Existing Rockwool Specifications on Claymont

Rockwool is already in the Claymont specification at three locations, established in earlier project work:

1. **Party wall blindside CI** — 2" minimum semi-rigid mineral wool, Rockwool CavityRock or equal, ≥ 4 lb/ft³ density, applied to interior face of 12" CMU party wall in each unit's garage interior, per Rockwool Technical Bulletin "Blindside Enclosure Walls in Zero Lot Line Conditions" (2024). Documented in Note Block 5B and 5C in the project general notes.

2. **Floor-line firestopping (head-of-wall)** — ThermaFiber Safing 45 or Rockwool Safe at the CMU-to-CLT and CMU-to-SIP transitions on Detail 2, 3, and 4 of A-502. Mechanically retained per A-502 review comment #15.

3. **Through-penetration firestop at CLT bearing on CMU** — mineral wool batt plus Hilti FS-ONE MAX at the CLT floor end, per UL HW-D-0969 / HW-D-0970 system listings.

Adding exterior CavityRock as continuous insulation outboard of the SIP envelope is an *incremental expansion* of Rockwool's role in the project, not a novel introduction. The supplier relationship is already established. The product family is already specified. The installation crew already has familiarity with the product. The grant narrative gains coherence from a single-supplier strategy across multiple envelope and firestop functions, as opposed to introducing a different manufacturer for the exterior CI layer.

This argues for the Claymont envelope upgrade described in Section 4 even at marginal cost, because the supply-chain and installation logic favors a Rockwool-integrated envelope strategy.

## 7. Recommendations

**One.** Do not specify SmartRock for any application in the Merge4Ward / M4D project pipeline that involves exterior CI over wood-framed sheathing. SmartRock is for interior CI on mass walls. Specify Rockwool Comfortboard 80/110 or CavityRock for exterior CI applications. Specify SmartRock only where the application matches its design intent — basement interior CI, mass-wall retrofit, or party-wall blindside CI conditions.

**Two.** Specify the article's exterior mineral wool buildup (with squash-block detail at appropriate fastener spacing) as a Merge4Ward standard wall option for Phase 2 product offerings. Use it as the default upgrade buildup for projects targeting Passive House, Living Building Challenge, IECC stretch-code compliance, or grant-funded deep retrofit work.

**Three.** Include the Claymont exterior mineral wool envelope upgrade as an additive alternate in the next reissue of the construction documents. If the GMP supports it, include it in the contracted scope. The grant narrative value alone (R-33 envelope, foam-free, vapor-open, fire-resistant) is worth the $10,000-$13,000 project cost. Coordinate with Travis on the A-502 detail revisions required to accommodate the additional layer.

**Four.** Do not pursue mineral-wool-core SIP development in Phase 1 or Phase 2. The structural and regulatory development pathway is multi-year and capital-intensive. Hold this as a Phase 4-5 R&D direction, potentially in partnership with an established European producer (per the ZÜBLIN strategic memo), and revisit the timing when Phase 1 manufacturing operations are profitable and the partnership conversations with European producers are mature.

**Five.** Document the existing Rockwool product specifications at Claymont (party wall blindside CI, floor-line firestop, through-penetration firestop) in a single project specification module so that the supply-chain relationship is visible as one integrated strategy rather than scattered across multiple notes. This supports the grant narrative and strengthens the Rockwool sales relationship for future Merge4Ward specification work.

## 8. Things to Watch

**Rockwool product line evolution.** Rockwool launched SmartRock in April 2025 and made it nationwide-available in February 2026. The pace of new product introduction at Rockwool over the past 24 months suggests the company is actively expanding its U.S. portfolio. A new exterior CI product specifically designed for the article's use case — possibly a higher-density CavityRock variant or a successor product — would not be a surprise within the next 18-24 months. Worth a quarterly check on Rockwool's product announcements.

**ICC-ES evaluation reports for novel SIP cores.** If any U.S. manufacturer brings a mineral-wool-core or wood-fiber-core structural panel to ICC-ES evaluation, that changes the Phase 4-5 calculus for Merge4Ward immediately. The novel-core-SIP evaluation pipeline at ICC-ES is small but growing. Worth tracking ICC-ES new evaluation reports quarterly for any panel-core innovation.

**STRABAG / ZÜBLIN North American expansion (per prior memo).** If ZÜBLIN announces a US partnership or facility, the LENO-ADD product line — which integrates CLT structure with mineral wool insulation — becomes a directly available product in the U.S. market, potentially through a Merge4Ward partnership channel. This collapses several of the Phase 2-5 product development questions into a partnership conversation. See prior ZÜBLIN strategic memo for full detail.

**IECC code cycle and stretch codes.** The 2024 IECC and the corresponding state and municipal code adoptions are tightening prescriptive R-value requirements progressively. R-20 + R-5 ci is the current 2018 IECC Climate Zone 4A baseline; the trend is toward R-20 + R-10 ci or higher in the 2024 cycle and beyond. Mineral wool exterior CI becomes more attractive as the prescriptive requirements rise, because the foam alternatives at R-10+ get expensive and continuity-of-thermal-control becomes harder. This is a tailwind for the Merge4Ward strategic direction described in this memo.
